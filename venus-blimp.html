Why about 56 km up

Venus's surface is 465°C under 92 bar of CO₂. Climb into the clouds and it cools steadily. Near 55–56 km the air is about 21°C and 0.5 bar, roughly the pressure at the top of Kilimanjaro.

Flying through acid

The clouds are droplets of 80–99% sulfuric acid, densest in the lower cloud near 48–50 km. Below about 47 km the droplets evaporate in the heat.

How it holds altitude

The sealed helium envelope floats at a fixed air density. A 2.5 kW compressor packs outside CO₂ into a tank at about 16 kg per hour to trim lower, and venting trims higher.

If the helium runs out

Once the envelope can't stay pressurized at night, it sinks. Nothing stops it before the 465°C surface, and each material fails as it passes its temperature limit.

The optimizer is a cross-entropy method, a simple machine-learning search. Each generation it samples envelope designs from probability distributions over the four layer materials, their thicknesses, the hull shape and the helium pressure. It sizes each design to float at the target temperature, then flies it through a full mission of acid, sunlight, helium leakage and fabric aging. The top 20% pull the distributions toward themselves, so the bars below show what it has learned.

Material assumptions

The optimizer can only be as right as this table. Acid ratings come from chemical-resistance charts (fluoropolymers rated excellent in all sulfuric acid strengths) and JPL tests. Erosion rates, helium permeabilities and aging rates are order-of-magnitude estimates, not measured Venus data. Acid rates are at the reference condition: 20 mg/m³ of 96% acid droplets at 25°C. They double for every 10°C hotter.

Atmosphere: approximate VIRA temperature profile, hydrostatic pressure from 92.1 bar at the surface; acid droplet density scaled to a published total cloud burden of about 0.22 kg/m² (densest in the lower cloud) and acid strength from about 80% at the cloud tops to 99% at the base. Flight: buoyancy, added mass, drag, slack-envelope behavior and burst; the blimp drifts with the wind on an equatorial track. Design lab: superpressure envelope sized to float at the target, Hoerner hull drag for cruise power, solar arrays at 40 W/kg (JPL's goal), batteries for the roughly 80-hour night. A simulation for intuition, not mission design.
